Why let the post office warm up?

Imagine judging a busy post office by its first customer of the day. The line is empty, so that customer gets unusually quick service. That is not a fair picture of a typical busy moment.

Warm-up lets customers arrive and the line develop before we start measuring. We keep the people in the system; we simply leave the opening minutes out of the averages.

Steady state does not mean a motionless queue. People still come and go, and the line still grows and shrinks. It means those ups and downs follow a pattern that no longer depends much on how the day began.

What was included in these measurements?

Number averages use the exact area under the event staircase. Service-station utilization uses actual occupied time. The postal-office illustration uses walking for visual continuity, but mathematical service ends when the receipt is stamped.

Little’s Law · connecting flow, time, and the line

Average customers ≈ arrivals per minute × average minutes per customer.

These are finite-run estimates, so the two sides need not match exactly. Some customers have not finished when time runs out. A longer stable run helps reduce opening and ending effects; repeated runs reveal random variation.

Random seed

A seed determines the random draws. Identical settings and seed reproduce the same mathematical arrivals and services, regardless of playback speed.

Warm-up and steady state, explained

The post office starts empty, so the first people get a lucky start. Warm-up gives the queue time to develop before we measure it. We do not clear the line when warm-up ends.

Steady state means the pattern of queue lengths has settled—not that the queue itself stops changing. Demand must be below total average capacity. A chosen warm-up does not prove that the pattern has settled, and near-capacity runs can take much longer.

Compare longer runs and different seeds before drawing conclusions about long-run performance.

A day at the post office

Customers come to mail a parcel. They join one shared line and wait for the next available service station. A clerk checks the address, weighs the parcel, processes payment, and stamps the receipt. Then the customer leaves.

Customers arrive randomly and service takes a random amount of time. What happens as demand approaches capacity?

Your analysis lab

Before starting, choose λ (arrivals/min), μ (completions/min per service station), and s (service stations). Those choices remain constant throughout the experiment. Total simulation time includes warm-up.

Arrivals follow a Poisson process. Times between arrivals and individual service times are independent exponential random variables. All service stations use the same average service rate. There is one unlimited, first-come, first-served line.

The timeline uses fixed 5-minute pages. Dots appear without moving across the page. The customer-count plot uses the same page. Pause to inspect earlier intervals or drag the counting window.
